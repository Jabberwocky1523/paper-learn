Abstract
KV caching reduces the serving cost of large language models
(LLMs), but the growing KV cache footprint is increasingly ex-
ceeds GPU and host-memory capacity. Therefore, people use SSD
to expand the KV cache storage capacity with high I/O bandwidth.
However, in production LLM workload, such as multi-round con-
Cache Hit Rate
versation and LLM agents, the total amount of KV cache writes
significantly exceeds SSD capacity, shortening SSD lifetime from
years to months. To improve SSD lifetime, a natural solution is to
reduce the KV cache writes by gating the KV cache writes via his-
torical access patterns. However, that significantly lowers the KV
cache hit rate (and increase the LLM serving cost) since KV caches
from different attention types might have identical historical access
pattern but different future access patterns. Our insight is that, to
reduce KV cache writes without hurting KV cache hit rate, one need
to not only consider the access pattern, but also application-level
information such as the attention type of KV cache. Leveraging this
observation, we propose Gandalf , the first SSD KV cache offload-
ing system with SSD life time awareness. Gandalf prioritize the
admission of full-attention KV cache into SSD over the cache gener-
ated by other types of attention, which significantly increases SSD
lifetime without sacrificing cache hit rate. Our evaluation shows
that Gandalf extends the SSD lifetime by 7.3× on average, while
having negligible impact on cache hit rate across multiple workload
traces and wide range of hardware capacity. Gandalf design can
be extended to other flash-based storage, such as High Bandwidth
Flash (HBF).
1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabili-
ties in multi-round conversations and agentic workflows, garnering
wide adoption. These workloads accumulate conversation histories,
tool outputs, and intermediate states, resulting in increasingly long
and frequently reused contexts. However, recomputing the atten-
tion key-value (KV) states for these contexts at every rounds sub-
stantially increases inference latency and cost. Hence, people store
all KV caches in their system to avoid recomputation and increase
cache hit rate [1, 32]. Production workloads generates Terabytes of
KV-cache per day at the amount that exceeds the capacity of GPU
and host memory, motivating the use of higher-capacity storage
tiers. Flash-based storage, e.g. NVMe SSDs, provides a scalable and
comparatively cost-effective tier for storing these caches [29].

This additional capacity comes with an endurance challenge
because SSDs can only handle a finite volume of writes before
they are worn out [4]. Under production workloads, aggressive KV-
cache admission improves cache-hit rate but generates substantial
write volume, exhausting SSD endurance within days to months.
Therefore, existing production-level systems came up with simple
filtering techniques that gates SSD admission, trading potential
cache hits for fewer writes. Frequency-based solutions, for example,
offloads a KV cache to SSD only if it has demonstrated 𝑁 × reuse,
with 𝑁 as a hyperparameter [30, 35]. However, even with such
filtering, the aggregate KV-cache write volume remains excessive
for practical SSD endurance. This raises a central question:
How can we improve the SSD lifetime without sacrificing KV caching
performance benefit?
The key challenge lies in predicting the future reuse of KV caches
such that we can selectively retain only the most reusable caches to
make the most of SSD’s capacity. Future prediction is difficult, but
we found application-specific property that strongly correlates with
reuse pattern. We leverage two observations: (1) Recent LLMs in-
creasingly employ hybrid architectures that combine full-attention
layers with linear- or sparse-attention layers, and (2) These dif-
ferent attention layer types produce KV caches with substantially
different reuse patterns under emerging mainstream workloads:

3.1 Attention type-aware placement
Hybrid models produce cached states with substantially different
sizes and reuse patterns. Full-attention layers retain per-token KV
entries, so their cache grows with the prefix length, whereas linear-
attention layers maintain a fixed-size recurrent state at each check-
point. Sliding-window layers retain only the most recent 𝑊 tokens
and can therefore use the same placement policy as linear-attention
layers. The state types also differ substantially in size. Across our
evaluated models, a single linear-attention state occupies as much
space as the full-attention KV entries for 2𝑘–16𝑘 tokens. However,
reuse is only meaningful when all types of states are available at
the same prefix boundary.
This heterogeneity in size and reuse pattern brings 2 opportu-
nities: (1) not all KV state needs to be cached, and (2) where an admitted state should be placed. The heterogeneity creates two
distinct cache-management decisions. First, admission determines
which generated states provide sufficient reuse value to retain. Sec-
ond, placement determines the storage tier in which each admitted
state should reside.
Prior works has solved “which KV state should be cached” by
probing the reuse boundary [33, 46]. These mechanisms address
which states should be retained, but they do not determine whether
an admitted state should reside in DRAM or in SSD. Our work is
aimed to improve SSD lifetime by managing KV cache placement
leveraging its attention layer type to give a hint on the most suitable
placement in the storage tier.
We therefore retain linear state in DRAM and full attention
KV caches in SSD. Each linear state generated at the end of each
conversation round is put in DRAM while the full attention KV
cache is put on SSD. When the storage tier (DRAM/SSD) is out of
space, the least-recently-used cache on the tier is evicted.

Capacity requirement for type-aware placement. The ideal
type-aware placement keeps linear-attention states in DRAM and
full-attention KV cache in SSD. In Equation 3, this corresponds to
𝑝 = 0, which maximizes the SSD lifetime improvement. However,
achieving this upper bound requires sufficient DRAM capacity to
retain the live linear states.
As observed in Section 2.6, in multi-round conversation and agen-
tic workloads, each session only needs to retain one linear-attention
state at a time, but it must retain all full-attention KV entries for
the entire prefix. Strict separation requires enough DRAM to hold
one live linear-attention state for every session and enough SSD
capacity to hold every session’s full-attention KV cache. As shown
in Figure 5, with the same number of sessions, the CPU/SSD capac-
ity ratio required to achieve maximum SSD-lifetime improvement
decreases with session length because the live linear state has fixed
size, whereas full-attention KV grows with the number of tokens.
Consequently, strict type-based placement cannot always use both
tiers efficiently. For short sessions, linear states dominate capac-
ity and some may need to borrow SSD space. For long sessions,
full-attention KV dominates and may benefit from borrowing un-
used DRAM space. We therefore design a capacity-aware placement
algorithm as an SSD admission gate that treats attention type as
a reuse pattern proxy for placement preference. The gate selects
which objects to move, retain, or evict based on their size, atten-
tion type-specific expected reuse, tier occupancy, and SSD-write
cost. We first study an offline optimal algorithm, approximate its
decisions, and then derive a practical online policy.

3.3.1 Dropping a DRAM candidate. Dropping an item misses the
cache hit opportunity, so it costs the recomputation of the dropped
object on its next read, which is the factor of number of hits 𝐻 𝑑 ,
the chunks have to be recomputed 𝑣 𝑑 , and the GPU cost to recom-

so Gandalf estimates it from the past history of requests. Three
observations make the estimation simple.
First, only the next read matters. A drop decision directly loses
the next hit. When it is reused again, the next read is recomputed
and admitted back to DRAM, where Gandalf decides its fate again.
When Gandalf decides its fate, it already pays for the recompu-
tation cost of the object that it causes to be evicted. We therefore
needs to know only whether 𝑑 will be read again, not how many
times. Linear attention states is simple to predict: as shown in Fig-
ure 6 most linear states are read once and then discarded, so 𝐻 𝑑
is either one or zero. We therefore only need to predict next reuse
probability for full attention KV chunks.
Second, returns are predictable from history. In the history, there
has been many requests being processed, and we can learn from
them to predict the future. Suppose the chunk’s session has been
idle for 𝑎 𝑑 requests. Gandalf asks: of the past sessions that were
also idle this long, how many came back? The chance of return is
the share that came back, denoted as 𝐻 𝑑 . It is close to 1 when the
predictor, based on the past experience with many requests, sure
that the cache will be read again, close to 0 otherwise.
Third, an object needed soon has a high value. Keeping 𝑑 is not
free: it holds 𝑠 𝑑 bytes of DRAM until its next read. Thus, evicting an
object that is needed farthest in the future is the cheapest, because
it frees the most DRAM for the longest time [3]. In the same light,
an object idle for long is likely to stay idle for long. Therefore,

Avoid writing non-reused linear states in SSD. Gandalf avoids

LRU-K at 𝐾 = 1 writes 120.6k linear states to the SSD, and only
21% of them are ever hit there: 43% are never requested again, and
45% had already served their one hit from DRAM before being
moved to the SSD, i.e., the state of a turn that the next turn already
reused. A larger 𝐾 does not fix this. LRU-K [35] counts references
per object, which captures hotness for objects that persist across
requests, such as a user’s KV cache in generative recommendation.

ratio rises from 0.1 to 0.14. Gandalf instead decides by whether

attention type as a proxy for reusability, which: (1) avoids writing
linear states at the end of each rounds to the SSD, (2) avoids writing

in DRAM and full-attention KV in the SSD.

5.8 How much is the cache load latency
affected?
We approximate the cache load latency of a request as the maxi-
mum of the time to transfer its cached KV to GPU HBM and the
time to recompute its missed tokens, since the two overlap. The
transfer uses DRAM as a staging buffer: KV on the SSD is first read
into DRAM (10GB/s), then every cached byte is sent to GPU HBM
(50GB/s); recomputation runs at 60k tokens/s. Figure 13 shows the
average cache load latency against SSD lifetime on ClaudeCode
with Kimi-K3, 100GB of DRAM and a 1TB SSD. The baselines with
the lowest load latency, Marconi at 𝛼 = 0 and LRU, load in 0.66s
and their SSDs last 4 months. Gandalf at 𝛼 = 1 lasts 4.2× longer
(17.0 months) at a load latency of 0.78s, 0.12s higher. It reads more
of its hits from the SSD, which is slower than DRAM, and its miss
ratio is 0.01 higher (0.056 against 0.045), so slightly more tokens are
recomputed. At a similar load latency, Gandalf lasts 3.7× longer
than LRU-K at 𝐾 = 2 (21.2 against 5.7 months, 0.79s against 0.81s),
and with 𝛼 = 64 it lasts 5.3× longer than LRU-K at 𝐾 = 3 while still
loading faster (38.6 against 7.3 months, 0.91s against 0.93s).
However, more recomputation is not always worse. Every write
wears out the SSD, effectively using a portion of its purchase price,
so the cost of a request is the GPU cost of recomputing its missed
tokens plus the SSD price share. Figure 14 shows this cost as an
average per request, choosing the cheapest hyperparameters for
each policy. SSD wear makes up 66–86% of the cost of LRU, Marconi
and LRU-K, and the cheapest baseline, LRU-K at 𝐾 = 2, costs $0.74
per 1k requests. Gandalf at 𝛼 = 1 costs $0.30 per 1k requests,
2.5× less: it recomputes more than LRU and Marconi ($0.13 against
$0.11 per 1k requests), but its SSD wear is 3.0× lower than that of
any baseline ($0.16 against $0.49–0.73).
