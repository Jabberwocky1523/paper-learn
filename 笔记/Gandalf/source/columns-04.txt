2.5 SSD lifetime and Cache Hit Rate Trade-off
Hardware configuration—size of host memory and SSD capacity—is
fixed. But workload demand can vary substantially over time. Since
cache entries are reused homogeneously, the storage hierarchy
must treat them uniformly. Hence, existing solutions trades off SSD
lifetime against cache hit rate, as shown in Figure 3.
Storing more KV-cache entries on SSD increases the probabil-
ity that future requests find reusable state, thereby improving the
cache-hit rate and reducing TTFT. However, the resulting write
volume rapidly consumes the SSD’s finite endurance budget. Con-
versely, storing fewer entries reduces SSD traffic and extends device

2.6 Key Observation: Hybrid model has
heterogeneous reuse pattern
Dense models use full attention across all layers. When a cached
context is resumed, the KV entries of every tokens in the sequence
prefix are reused. Thus, storing a larger portion of the prefix allows
more recomputation to be avoided, creating a near one-for-one
trade-off between SSD writes and cache-hit rate. Each layer and
token’s admitted KV entry provides comparable reuse value, and
reducing SSD writes generally sacrifices a corresponding caching
opportunity. This creates the apparent one-for-one trade-off be-
tween SSD lifetime and cache-hit rate.
Hybrid models break this trade-off due to its model architec-
ture: combining full-attention layers with sparse attention layers,
such as linear and sliding-window attention. As shown in Table 1,
cached states from different layer types exhibit substantially non-
uniform reuse patterns, where linear state is reused much less
than full attention KV cache. We observed that under append-only
workloads such as multi-round conversation, all prefix tokens’ full-
attention KV caches are reused in every subsequent round, so their
reuse count increases as the conversation grows. By contrast, each
sparse-attention state is typically used only once—to generate the
immediately following round—before being superseded by a newer
state.
Uniformly writing these short-lived states to SSD is inefficient.
Write-through immediately persists them, while write-back may
persist them upon eviction, even though they are likely to be super-
seded after only one reuse. When the latest linear-attention state
can remain in DRAM until the next round, its SSD copy provides no
additional cache hit but still consumes SSD endurance and capacity.
Such writes may also evict more reusable full-attention KV entries,
further reducing cache effectiveness.
