This reuse pattern heterogeneity motivates attention-type-aware
cache placement. Writing large, one-time use linear-attention states
to SSD provides little cache hit benefit while consuming both write
endurance and cache capacity. Layer type can therefore serve as
a practical placement signal, allowing us to utilize SSDs to ex-
pand cache capacity while minimizing endurance loss. We propose
an attention layer-aware placement policy that caches short-lived
linear-attention states in host memory while keeping longer-lived
full-attention KV caches on SSD.
However, there are 2 practical challenges that entail this place-
ment policy.
First, this placement policy is too rigid: the size of CPU DRAM
and SSD is fixed, but workload can generate different amounts
of different attention states. Particularly, a workload with many
concurrent sessions can generate a large number of reusable linear-
attention states that exceeds the available DRAM capacity, simi-
larly, a workload with long contexts can generate a large number
of reusable full-attention KV caches that exceeds the available SSD
capacity. We call this a hardware-to-utilization imbalance. This
imbalance forces frequent cache eviction on the saturated tier de-
spite available capacity elsewhere in the storage hierarchy, thus
lowering cache-hit rate. Worse, if the overutilization happens in
SSD, the resulting write churn can consume its endurance budget
more rapidly and further shorten its lifetime. Therefore, we design
a capacity-aware placement policy that filters SSD admission based
on utilization and pressure of each tier, balancing cache hit rate
against SSD endurance.
Second, such rigid placement moves cache hit from DRAM to SSD,
which can hurt TTFT because SSD bandwidth is substantially lower
than DRAM bandwidth. Hence, transferring large full-attention KV
caches from SSD may hurt TTFT.
We explore the potential of attention-type-aware placement us-
ing an offline oracle that jointly optimizes SSD admission and cache
eviction decisions, biased towards our tier placement. The oracle
reveals two complementary design principles. First, placement must
adapt to tier capacity and selectively persist KV caches on SSD to
preserve the prefix-hit boundary. Second, admission and eviction
must be optimized jointly: admitting a new cache has cost and ben-
efit. It benefits future reuse, but it has write cost and replacement
cost—cost of replacing other cache entries, may or may not already
pay for its promised benefit. An SSD admission is beneficial only
when its expected reuse value exceeds the sum of write cost and
the value of the entries it evicts. Together, these principles charac-
terize the best attainable trade-off among SSD lifetime, cache-hit
rate, and TTFT. We then distill the oracle’s decisions into an online,
capacity-aware policy that estimates future reuse from historical
access patterns.

Contributions. Our work makes the following contributions:
(1) We are the first to show that the SSD lifetime is significantly
lower than its factory guarantee in production-scale SSD-
based KV cache offloading.
(2) We show that, leveraging application-level information (the
attention type corresponds to each KV cache objects), we
can significantly reduce the write volume and increase SSD
life time with negligible impact on KV cache hit rate.
(3) We propose Gandalf , a KV caching system that can in-
crease SSD lifetime by 7.3× (on average) with negligible
impact on hit rate across a wide range of LLMs and hard-
ware capacity.
Although this paper only demonstrates Gandalf on SSD, but
the technique is extendable to other flash-based storage devices,
such as High Bandwidth Flash (HBF).
2 Motivation
2.1 KV Caches Exceed Memory Capacity
Over the past years, the increasing intelligence and context window
size of LLMs empowered multi-round conversations and agentic
workflows. These types of workloads continuously accumulate
conversation history, intermediate reasoning, tool outputs, and
subagent results. Unlike single-turn requests, these workloads re-
peatedly extend and reuse an existing prefix, causing each session’s
KV-cache footprint to grow throughout its lifetime. Hence, the
aggregate KV cache sizes saved across requests and sessions are
getting enormous.
Long contexts also make cache misses increasingly expensive.
When a reusable prefix is not cached, the serving system must
recompute its KV states by prefilling all preceding tokens before
generating a response. In workloads with long context sizes, such
as multi-round conversation, agentic workflow, and long document
processing, this repeatedly consumes scarce GPU computation,
increasing TTFT and serving cost. People therefore seek to retain
as much reusable KV state as possible, effectively trading storage
capacity for computation.
