for LLM serving [13, 16, 28, 38, 41, 43]. Since SSD is known to have a

2.3 KV Caching is Rapidly Killing SSDs
Under frontier model and agentic workloads, our analysis shows
that even a 30-TB enterprise SSD can exhaust its rated TBW in
less than one year. Smaller enterprise SSDs wear out even faster,
with a 2-TB drive reaching its rated TBW in just days. In §2.2, we
discussed that SSD wear is a product of host-write volume and
device-level write amplification. We target host writes by reducing
the KV-cache writes issued to SSD.
Tiered KV caches commonly use write-through or write-back
offloading.
Write-through immediately copies every admitted KV states to
SSD, maximizing availability at multiple tiers, but generating sub-
stantial SSD write. This duplication preserves the entry after DRAM
eviction, but wastes SSD endurance when all of its reuse occurs in a
short time while it remains in DRAM. The SSD copy then consumes
write budget without serving a cache hit.
Write-back persists demoted KV states from higher storage tier
regardless of its expected reuse. This policy still writes every DRAM
eviction to SSD, including states that contribute little to future cache
hits.
Selective write-through reduces this traffic using a fixed reuse
threshold; for example, NVIDIA Dynamo offloads a block only after
its access frequency reaches two [30]. However, frequency-only
filtering ignores state size, layer-specific reuse, tier occupancy, and
the remaining write budget, allowing large, short-lived states to
consume substantial endurance for little benefit.
2.4 How does prior work address SSD
endurance?
SSD endurance has long been a concern in flash-based caching
systems. Traditional systems, such as Flashcache [11] and Google’s
Colossus Flash Cache [51], use SSDs to accelerate access to persis-
tent data. Recent studies show that KV-cache offloading introduces
similar endurance challenges in LLM serving [23, 42]. Existing
approaches can be grouped into three categories.
